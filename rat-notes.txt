Two fundamentally different approaches — pick one, it changes everything downstream:

Gray-box / parametric — assume the pedal is a small chain of known DSP blocks (a waveshaper for Distortion, a filter for Filter), and fit each block's parameters (drive amount, cutoff, Q, gain staging) from your captured audio at each of the 7 positions. Interpolate/extrapolate between positions with a curve fit (e.g. cutoff frequency vs. knob position). This maps cleanly onto juce::dsp::WaveShaper + juce::dsp::IIR/StateVariableTPTFilter, is cheap at runtime, and gives you continuous knob behavior instead of just 49 presets.
Black-box / neural — train a small conditioned network (WaveNet-lite, LSTM, or similar — RTNeural is the common JUCE-compatible inference runtime) on input→output pairs, conditioned on the two knob values. More faithful to weird nonlinear/dynamic behavior, but needs far more and more varied training audio than 49 static snapshots, and a training pipeline outside JUCE.

My default recommendation is gray-box unless the pedal has genuinely idiosyncratic behavior (bias wobble, dynamic compression, interactive knob coupling) that a simple clipper+filter can't capture — it's much less data-hungry and easier to debug/tune by ear.

On your signal-order question: yes, it matters a lot, and only in the gray-box case. Nonlinear waveshaping and filtering don't commute — clip-then-filter and filter-then-clip produce different harmonic/intermodulation content, especially near the filter's cutoff. Good news: this is usually discoverable rather than guessable. The overwhelmingly common analog topology (Tube Screamer, RAT, Big Muff, etc.) is input buffer → clipping stage → tone/filter stack → output/volume, so that's the reasonable prior. You can confirm it empirically from your captures by comparing harmonic signatures at extreme settings (e.g. Distortion=6, Filter=0 vs Filter=6) against both orderings implemented in a quick offline test. (If it's black-box, order is irrelevant — the network just learns the end-to-end function.)

One thing that'll matter a lot for quality regardless of approach: if your 49 samples are all the same test signal (e.g. one riff), the model will likely overfit to that specific input. You'll get much better generalization if the captures include varied dynamics — single notes at different velocities, chords, palm mutes, sustained notes — especially for the Distortion knob, since clipping behavior is level-dependent.

But the per-band table exposes a real problem: high-frequency rolloff to literal digital silence. Look at the pattern — real and predicted agree closely through 1kHz (within 0.1dB), start diverging by 2-3kHz, and by 8kHz the predicted output is 15dB quieter. Above 15kHz, every single predicted value is exactly -150.0 dB — that's not "very quiet," that's the numerical floor of the calculation itself (10*log10(1e-15) = -150.0 exactly, from the epsilon added to avoid log(0)). The model's output has become bit-exact zero above ~10-15kHz, while the real pedal still carries genuine energy (even if near its own noise floor around -134dB) all the way to 45kHz.

That's a real, diagnosable model limitation, not measurement noise: the model isn't generating the pedal's high-frequency harmonic content at all. A RAT-style diode clipper genuinely produces rich harmonics extending well into the upper spectrum — a model that rolls off to true silence above 10kHz is under-representing the distortion itself, not just being slightly imprecise. hidden_size=20 may be too small to capture the fast nonlinear waveshaping needed for full harmonic generation at 96kHz.
